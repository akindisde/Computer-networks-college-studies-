# Chapter 3: Message Authentication Codes (MACs) and Hash Functions

Message authentication is the natural continuation of confidentiality. In the previous chapter, we studied how symmetric encryption can prevent an adversary from learning the content of a message. That protection, however, does not automatically prevent an adversary from modifying the message. A ciphertext can remain unreadable while being maliciously altered.

This chapter develops the cryptographic mechanisms used to detect such unauthorized modifications. We begin with active attacks and the distinction between confidentiality and integrity. We then introduce Message Authentication Codes (MACs), formalize their security, and construct MACs from pseudorandom functions and permutations. Because practical messages are not restricted to a fixed block size, we study constructions such as CBC-MAC, NMAC, PMAC, and HMAC. We then introduce existential forgery, which is the central failure condition for a MAC.

The second major part of the chapter introduces cryptographic hash functions. We study their definitions, security requirements, construction techniques, the Merkle–Damgård paradigm, and constructions based on PRFs and PRPs. Finally, we examine representative attacks against MACs and hash functions. Throughout the chapter, the main objective is to understand not only how these primitives work, but also what security property each construction is actually designed to provide.

---

## 1. The Problem of Active Attacks

Cryptographic security is often introduced by considering a passive adversary. A passive adversary observes communications but does not alter them. Confidentiality mechanisms such as encryption are designed primarily to resist this type of observation by preventing an attacker from learning the plaintext.

A much more powerful adversary is an **active adversary**. Instead of merely observing communication, the attacker can inject, delete, replay, reorder, or modify messages. The attacker may be able to intercept a legitimate message, alter selected bits, and forward the result to the recipient. The attacker may also construct an entirely new message and attempt to make the receiver accept it as authentic.

This distinction is fundamental because encryption alone does not necessarily detect modification.

Suppose a sender transmits an encrypted message

$$  
C=\operatorname{Enc}_K(M).  
$$

An attacker intercepts $C$ and replaces it with $C'$. If the receiver simply decrypts

$$  
M'=\operatorname{Dec}_K(C'),  
$$

there is no general reason for the receiver to know whether $C'$ was generated by the legitimate sender or by the attacker.

The attacker may not know the key $K$, and may still be unable to recover $M$. Nevertheless, the attacker may be able to manipulate the ciphertext in ways that produce meaningful effects after decryption.

This illustrates a crucial distinction in cryptographic engineering: **confidentiality and integrity are separate security properties**.

Confidentiality asks whether an unauthorized party can learn information about a protected message. Integrity asks whether an unauthorized party can modify or create data without being detected.

The difference can be illustrated conceptually by considering a bank transaction. Encryption may prevent an attacker from reading the transaction "transfer $100 from Alice to Bob." But if the attacker can modify it into "transfer $10,000 from Alice to Bob" and the recipient accepts the altered message, confidentiality has succeeded while integrity has failed.

Active attacks therefore require authentication mechanisms in addition to encryption.

Another active attack is **replay**. Suppose a legitimate message is

$$  
M=\text{“Transfer \$100.”}  
$$

and the attacker records the valid encrypted and authenticated message. Even if the attacker cannot modify it, the attacker might transmit the same valid message again. The receiver could interpret the replay as a new instruction.

A MAC can establish that a message was generated by someone possessing the secret key, but it does not automatically establish freshness or prevent replay. Replay protection generally requires additional protocol state, sequence numbers, timestamps, nonces, counters, or application-level mechanisms.

This observation is important when analyzing real protocols: cryptographic primitives provide specific properties, while protocols combine those primitives to achieve larger security goals.

## 2. The Need for Message Integrity Protection Compared with Confidentiality

Confidentiality and integrity protect against different classes of threats.

**Confidentiality** attempts to prevent unauthorized disclosure:

$$  
M \longrightarrow C  
$$

should hide useful information about $M$ from an attacker who sees $C$.

**Integrity**, by contrast, requires that an attacker cannot cause an altered message to be accepted as authentic.

Suppose a sender and receiver share a secret key $K$. A confidentiality mechanism might provide

$$  
C=\operatorname{Enc}_K(M).  
$$

A message authentication mechanism instead produces an authentication tag:

$$  
T=\operatorname{MAC}_K(M).  
$$

The sender transmits something conceptually equivalent to

$$  
(M,T).  
$$

The receiver computes

$$  
T'=\operatorname{MAC}_K(M)  
$$

and accepts the message only if

$$  
T'=T.  
$$

The crucial difference is that an attacker who does not know $K$ should be unable to produce a valid tag for a modified message.

The tag does not need to conceal the message. A MAC is not an encryption algorithm. Its purpose is to provide a compact cryptographic authenticator demonstrating knowledge of a shared secret.

The security properties of a MAC therefore differ from those of encryption. Encryption is designed to hide information; a MAC is designed to make unauthorized modification or forgery computationally infeasible.

In modern systems, confidentiality and integrity are frequently combined through **authenticated encryption**. A construction such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 encrypts the plaintext while also generating an authentication tag.

The conceptual construction is

$$  
(C,T)\leftarrow\operatorname{Enc}_K(N,M,A),  
$$

where $N$ is a nonce and $A$ is associated data. The ciphertext $C$ is confidential, while both $C$ and $A$ are authenticated.

The distinction between confidentiality and integrity also explains why simply "encrypting something" is not enough to make it secure. Encryption without authentication may leave an active attacker with opportunities to modify ciphertexts, exploit malleability, or manipulate protocol behavior.

A secure cryptographic protocol must therefore identify explicitly which security properties are required. If unauthorized modification must be detected, some form of integrity protection is necessary.

## 3. MACs: Construction and Security

A **Message Authentication Code (MAC)** is a symmetric cryptographic primitive that allows a sender and receiver sharing a secret key to authenticate messages.

A MAC scheme consists conceptually of three algorithms:

$$  
K\leftarrow\operatorname{Gen}(1^\lambda),  
$$

$$  
T\leftarrow\operatorname{Tag}_K(M),  
$$

and

$$  
b\leftarrow\operatorname{Verify}_K(M,T).  
$$

The verification algorithm returns acceptance or rejection. Correctness requires that a tag generated honestly is accepted:

$$  
\operatorname{Verify}_K(M,\operatorname{Tag}_K(M))=1.  
$$

The security requirement is more subtle. The attacker should not be able to generate a valid tag for a new message without knowing the secret key.

The MAC tag can be thought of as a cryptographic checksum, but this analogy must be used carefully. Ordinary checksums are designed to detect accidental errors. A MAC must resist a deliberate adversary who knows the construction and may intentionally choose modifications designed to produce a valid-looking result.

This difference is fundamental. If an attacker knows the checksum algorithm and can freely calculate checksums, a checksum does not authenticate the source of a message. A MAC uses a secret key so that only authorized parties can efficiently generate valid tags.

### MAC Security Through Indistinguishability

It is useful to distinguish MAC security from encryption security. For encryption, a central goal is indistinguishability of ciphertexts. For a MAC, the central concern is **unforgeability**.

A standard security experiment gives the adversary access to a tagging oracle. The adversary can submit messages

$$  
M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_q  
$$

and receive corresponding tags

$$  
T_i=\operatorname{Tag}_K(M_i).  
$$

The adversary then outputs a candidate pair

$$  
(M^_,T^_).  
$$

The attack succeeds if

$$  
\operatorname{Verify}_K(M^_,T^_)=1  
$$

and $M^*$ was not previously submitted to the tagging oracle.

The security goal is that every efficient adversary should have only negligible probability of producing such a forgery.

This security model is powerful because it assumes the attacker can obtain valid authentication tags for messages of its own choice. A secure MAC must remain secure even when the attacker has substantial interaction with the legitimate authentication mechanism.

The MAC key must remain secret. The attacker is assumed to know the MAC algorithm itself. This follows the fundamental cryptographic principle that security should depend on the secrecy of keys rather than on the secrecy of algorithms.

### MACs and Authentication

A successful MAC verification demonstrates knowledge of the secret key under the assumptions of the construction. This is often described as message authentication.

It is important not to interpret this as a complete proof of the identity of a human being or device. If several systems share the same key, any one of them can generate valid tags. Furthermore, if the key is compromised, an attacker can create authentic-looking messages.

MAC authentication is therefore fundamentally tied to **key management**.

## 4. MACs from PRFs and PRPs: Construction and Security

A powerful way to construct a MAC is to use a **pseudorandom function (PRF)**.

Let

$$  
F_K:{0,1}^n\rightarrow{0,1}^t  
$$

be a secure PRF. A simple fixed-length MAC can be defined as

$$  
\operatorname{Tag}_K(M)=F_K(M).  
$$

The intuition is that the tag behaves like the output of a random function that only someone possessing $K$ can evaluate.

An adversary can query the tagging oracle on chosen messages. If the underlying function is pseudorandom, the adversary should not be able to predict the correct output for a new message except with probability close to that obtained by guessing.

For a tag of $t$ bits, a random guess succeeds with probability approximately

$$  
2^{-t}.  
$$

This gives an important design relationship between tag length and forgery resistance. If a system permits an attacker to make many attempts, the cumulative probability of successful guessing must also be considered.

Suppose the attacker makes $q$ independent random attempts. A rough upper-bound intuition is

$$  
\Pr[\text{success}]  
\lesssim  
\frac{q}{2^t}.  
$$

Therefore, a short tag can become dangerous when an attacker is permitted a large number of verification attempts.

A PRP, such as a secure block cipher, can also be used as a building block. Because a PRP is a permutation rather than a general function, additional structure is often needed when constructing a MAC for arbitrary messages.

The distinction between PRFs and PRPs is therefore central. A PRF behaves like a random function; a PRP behaves like a random permutation. Block ciphers such as AES are naturally modeled as PRPs, while constructions such as HMAC are more naturally analyzed through PRF-like assumptions.

## 5. MACs for Arbitrary-Length Messages: CBC-MAC, NMAC, PMAC, and HMAC

The simple construction

$$  
\operatorname{Tag}_K(M)=F_K(M)  
$$

assumes that $M$ fits the input domain of the function. Real messages can be arbitrarily long, so a MAC must define how a sequence of blocks is processed.

This is where constructions such as CBC-MAC, NMAC, PMAC, and HMAC become important.

### CBC-MAC

CBC-MAC applies the chaining principle of CBC mode to message authentication.

Suppose the message is divided into blocks

$$  
M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_\ell.  
$$

Set

$$  
C_0=0^n.  
$$

Then compute

$$  
C_i=E_K(M_i\oplus C_{i-1})  
$$

for each block. The final block

$$  
T=C_\ell  
$$

is used as the authentication tag.

The construction is elegant, but its security depends critically on message-length restrictions and how the scheme is used.

For fixed-length messages, CBC-MAC can be secure under appropriate assumptions. For variable-length messages, the naive construction is vulnerable to extension-style attacks because an attacker may combine valid MAC computations in ways that produce valid tags for related messages.

This teaches a general lesson: **a construction secure for fixed-length messages is not automatically secure for arbitrary-length messages**.

Variants such as encrypted CBC-MAC and other strengthened constructions were developed to address these issues, but modern protocols generally prefer well-analyzed constructions such as HMAC or dedicated authenticated-encryption schemes.

### NMAC

**NMAC** is a nested MAC construction based on a compression function or keyed primitive.

At a high level, the message is processed under an inner key, producing an intermediate result, and then that result is processed under an outer key:

F_{K_2}(F_{K_1}(M)).  
$$

The actual construction depends on the underlying iterated function and message processing mechanism.

NMAC is important theoretically because it provides a clean bridge between hash-function constructions and MAC security. It also provides the conceptual foundation for understanding HMAC.

### PMAC

**PMAC**, or Parallelizable MAC, was designed to overcome one limitation of CBC-style authentication: sequential processing.

PMAC processes message blocks in parallel using block-cipher operations combined with distinct masks. A simplified conceptual representation is

$$  
X_i=E_K(M_i\oplus \Delta_i),  
$$

where each $\Delta_i$ is derived in a key-dependent way so that different blocks are processed under distinct effective offsets.

The resulting values are combined to produce the final tag.

The parallel structure is particularly useful on modern hardware because independent message blocks can be processed simultaneously.

### HMAC

**HMAC**, or Hash-based Message Authentication Code, is one of the most widely deployed MAC constructions.

Given a cryptographic hash function $H$, HMAC is defined conceptually as

H\left((K'\oplus opad)\parallel  
H((K'\oplus ipad)\parallel M)\right),  
$$

where $K'$ is the normalized key and $ipad$ and $opad$ are fixed padding constants.

The inner computation is

$$  
H((K'\oplus ipad)\parallel M),  
$$

and the outer computation is

$$  
H((K'\oplus opad)\parallel \text{inner result}).  
$$

The two-layer structure is essential. HMAC is not simply

$$  
H(K\parallel M).  
$$

That simpler construction can suffer from weaknesses related to the internal structure of Merkle–Damgård hash functions.

HMAC is particularly important because it is built from widely available cryptographic hash functions and has a strong security analysis. It has been deployed in network protocols, APIs, authentication systems, and key-derivation mechanisms.

The existence of HMAC also illustrates an important principle: a hash function that is not itself keyed can nevertheless become a secure keyed authentication mechanism when embedded in a carefully designed construction.

## 6. Existential Forgery and Security Conditions

The most important security notion for a MAC is resistance to **existential forgery under chosen-message attack**.

The phrase "existential forgery" means that the attacker does not need to forge a specific meaningful message. It is enough to produce **any new message and a valid tag** that were not previously authenticated.

This may initially seem weak. In practice, however, the ability to create even one valid authenticated message can be extremely dangerous. The attacker might exploit a protocol command, alter a transaction, inject a control message, or create malformed input that triggers a vulnerability.

The standard security experiment proceeds as follows. The attacker receives access to a tagging oracle:

$$  
T_i=\operatorname{Tag}_K(M_i).  
$$

The attacker may choose the messages adaptively. After obtaining as many valid examples as desired within the allowed query limit, the attacker outputs

$$  
(M^_,T^_).  
$$

The adversary wins if

$$  
\operatorname{Verify}_K(M^_,T^_)=1  
$$

and $M^*$ was not among the messages submitted previously.

The probability of this event defines the adversary's forgery success probability.

A secure MAC requires this probability to be negligible for every efficient adversary, subject to the intended security bounds.

### Tag Length

Tag length is a major security parameter. If a tag contains only $t$ bits, an attacker who has no better strategy can guess one with probability

$$  
2^{-t}.  
$$

If the attacker can make $q$ independent attempts, the success probability can become approximately

$$  
1-(1-2^{-t})^q,  
$$

which for relatively small $q/2^t$ is approximately

$$  
\frac{q}{2^t}.  
$$

This is why authentication systems must limit verification attempts and select sufficiently long tags.

### Stronger Notions

A MAC security definition may also specify whether the attacker can obtain tags adaptively, whether tags may be truncated, whether multiple keys are used, and whether the message domain has special structure.

These details matter because cryptographic security is always relative to a model. A statement such as "HMAC is secure" is incomplete unless we specify what property is being claimed and under what assumptions.

### MACs Do Not Automatically Provide Non-Repudiation

A MAC proves, under the security model, that someone possessing the shared secret could have generated the tag. It does not provide public verifiability or non-repudiation because both sender and receiver know the same secret key.

Digital signatures address a different problem. A signature can be verified using a public key and can therefore provide stronger properties concerning public attribution. MACs are instead designed for efficient shared-secret authentication.

## 7. Cryptographic Hash Functions: Definitions, Security Conditions, and Construction Methods

A **cryptographic hash function** is a deterministic function that maps inputs of arbitrary length to fixed-length outputs.

We can write

$$  
H:{0,1}^*\rightarrow{0,1}^n.  
$$

The notation ${0,1}^*$ means that the input can have any finite length, while the output always has exactly $n$ bits.

The output is called a **digest**, **hash value**, or **hash**.

Hash functions are not encryption algorithms. There is normally no secret key and no efficient decryption algorithm. Their purpose is to compress arbitrary-length data into a fixed-size representation while satisfying security properties that make certain inverse or collision-related attacks computationally difficult.

### Preimage Resistance

Given a target hash value

$$  
y=H(M),  
$$

a preimage-resistant hash function should make it computationally infeasible to find any message $M'$ such that

$$  
H(M')=y.  
$$

For an ideal $n$-bit hash function, generic preimage search requires approximately

$$  
2^n  
$$

work.

### Second-Preimage Resistance

Given a message $M$, it should be difficult to find a different message $M'\neq M$ satisfying

$$  
H(M')=H(M).  
$$

This is called **second-preimage resistance**.

The generic complexity for an ideal $n$-bit hash is also approximately

$$  
2^n.  
$$

### Collision Resistance

A collision is a pair of distinct messages

$$  
M\neq M'  
$$

such that

$$  
H(M)=H(M').  
$$

Collision resistance requires that finding any such pair be computationally infeasible.

For an ideal $n$-bit hash function, the birthday paradox implies a generic collision attack requiring approximately

$$  
2^{n/2}  
$$

hash evaluations.

This difference is essential:

$$  
\text{preimage security}\approx 2^n,  
$$

while

$$  
\text{collision security}\approx 2^{n/2}.  
$$

For SHA-256, for example, the generic collision-security level is approximately $2^{128}$ rather than $2^{256}$.

### Hash Functions and MACs

A hash function by itself does not authenticate a message because everyone can compute it. An attacker can calculate

$$  
H(M')  
$$

for any chosen $M'$.

A MAC introduces a secret key:

$$  
T=\operatorname{MAC}_K(M).  
$$

Therefore, hash functions and MACs should not be confused. A cryptographic hash function provides properties such as collision resistance and preimage resistance, while a MAC provides keyed authentication and unforgeability.

### Construction Approaches

Cryptographic hash functions can be built using different approaches. Some constructions are based on iterated compression functions, some use dedicated mathematical structures, and some are based on permutations or sponge constructions.

Historically, the Merkle–Damgård paradigm has been extremely influential. Modern families such as SHA-2 use related iterated designs, while SHA-3 uses a fundamentally different sponge construction.

Hash-function design also relies on mathematical structures such as finite-field arithmetic, modular arithmetic, Boolean functions, permutations, and carefully designed nonlinear transformations.

## 8. Merkle–Damgård Constructions, PRF/PRP-Based Constructions, and Security Conditions

The **Merkle–Damgård construction** is a general method for transforming a fixed-input-length compression function into a hash function that can process messages of arbitrary length.

The message is divided into blocks:

$$  
M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_\ell.  
$$

An initial chaining value is selected:

$$  
H_0=IV.  
$$

A compression function $f$ is then iterated:

$$  
H_i=f(H_{i-1},M_i).  
$$

After processing the final block,

$$  
H(M)=H_\ell.  
$$

The basic idea is powerful because a fixed-size compression function can be repeatedly applied to an arbitrary number of message blocks.

### Padding

The message generally needs to be padded so that its length becomes a multiple of the block size. A common design also encodes the original message length in the padding.

Length encoding is important because careless padding can create structural ambiguities. A secure iterated hash construction must define its message encoding unambiguously.

### Merkle–Damgård Security Intuition

Under appropriate assumptions about the compression function, Merkle–Damgård can preserve important collision-resistance properties. Informally, if an attacker finds a collision in the final hash, the structure can often be used to derive a collision in one of the compression steps.

However, the construction has important structural properties that must be understood carefully.

One of the most important is the **length-extension property**. If

$$  
H(M)  
$$

is known for a Merkle–Damgård hash and the attacker knows the length of $M$, the attacker may be able to compute a valid hash related to

$$  
M\parallel \operatorname{pad}(M)\parallel X  
$$

without knowing $M$ itself.

This is one reason why simply constructing a MAC as

$$  
H(K\parallel M)  
$$

is not generally considered safe. HMAC was designed to avoid this problem through its nested construction.

### PRF/PRP-Based Hash Constructions

Hash functions can also be constructed from pseudorandom primitives. A block cipher or PRP can be used within a compression function, with appropriate domain separation and chaining rules.

The general principle is that a secure keyed primitive can provide a strong source of pseudorandomness or unpredictability from which other cryptographic constructions can be built.

However, the fact that a construction uses AES or another secure PRP does not automatically make the resulting hash secure. The exact composition matters. Security properties must be proved or carefully analyzed for the entire construction.

This is a recurring theme in cryptography:

$$  
\text{secure primitive}  
+  
\text{incorrect composition}  
\neq  
\text{secure system}.  
$$

### Sponge Construction

Although the Merkle–Damgård construction is central to this chapter, modern hash-function design also includes the **sponge construction**, which is used by SHA-3.

A sponge has an internal state divided conceptually into a rate portion and a capacity portion. Input blocks are absorbed into the state, and output blocks are subsequently squeezed from it.

The security level depends strongly on the capacity. This architecture differs fundamentally from Merkle–Damgård and avoids some of its characteristic structural properties.

Understanding the difference between these constructions is important when studying modern cryptographic hash functions.

## 9. Attacks on MACs and Hash Functions

Cryptographic attacks are not limited to brute-force searches. Many important attacks exploit structural properties, incorrect assumptions, implementation errors, or inappropriate composition.

### Brute-Force MAC Forgery

If a MAC tag has $t$ bits and the attacker cannot exploit a structural weakness, the simplest attack is to guess a tag.

A single attempt succeeds with probability approximately

$$  
2^{-t}.  
$$

After $q$ attempts, the success probability is approximately

$$  
\frac{q}{2^t}  
$$

when the probability is small.

This explains why tag truncation must be performed carefully and why systems should limit failed authentication attempts.

### CBC-MAC Extension Attacks

Naive CBC-MAC becomes insecure when used with variable-length messages without appropriate strengthening.

Suppose an attacker knows a valid tag for one message and can manipulate the chaining structure. Because CBC-MAC's internal state becomes the input to the next block, an attacker may construct a related longer message whose tag can be derived from existing valid information.

This is a classic example of a construction being secure under a restricted domain but insecure when its domain assumptions are violated.

The lesson is general: **never extend a fixed-length MAC construction to arbitrary-length messages without a proof or a standard construction designed for that purpose**.

### Length-Extension Attacks

Merkle–Damgård hashes can expose a length-extension property. If the attacker knows

$$  
H(M),  
$$

and knows the length of $M$, the attacker may be able to compute a hash corresponding to

$$  
M\parallel\operatorname{pad}(M)\parallel X  
$$

for chosen $X$, without learning $M$.

This attack does not mean that the underlying hash function is "broken" in the sense of collision resistance. It means that the hash's structural property makes certain naive constructions unsafe.

The classic example is the naive MAC

$$  
H(K\parallel M).  
$$

HMAC avoids this problem through its nested keyed construction.

### Birthday Attacks

Because an $n$-bit hash has only

$$  
2^n  
$$

possible outputs, collisions become likely after roughly

$$  
2^{n/2}  
$$

random evaluations.

This is the birthday phenomenon.

An attacker can therefore search for a collision much more efficiently than by trying to invert a particular hash output. This is why collision resistance must be considered separately from preimage resistance.

### Weak Hash Functions

MD5 provides an important historical example. Its collision resistance has been practically broken, and it should not be used for cryptographic collision-resistant applications.

SHA-1 has similarly suffered practical collision attacks and should no longer be considered suitable for applications requiring strong collision resistance.

These examples demonstrate the difference between an algorithm that was once considered secure and an algorithm that remains secure under current cryptanalytic knowledge.

### Differential Attacks

Many cryptographic constructions can be analyzed through differences in inputs and outputs. **Differential cryptanalysis** studies how controlled differences propagate through a cryptographic function.

For hash functions and compression functions, an attacker may search for differential characteristics that cause internal differences to cancel or follow unusually favorable paths.

For MAC constructions based on block ciphers or compression functions, structural weaknesses in the underlying primitive or its composition can sometimes produce forgery strategies.

### Collision-Based Attacks Against MAC Constructions

A collision in a public hash function does not automatically imply a MAC forgery against HMAC. The keyed construction changes the attack surface.

This distinction is important. If an attacker can find

$$  
H(M)=H(M')  
$$

without knowing a key, that does not necessarily mean the attacker can produce

$$  
\operatorname{HMAC}_K(M)  
$$

and

$$  
\operatorname{HMAC}_K(M')  
$$

for arbitrary messages.

The security of HMAC depends on the keyed construction and assumptions about the underlying hash function, not merely on whether collisions exist in the public hash.

### Key-Recovery and Side-Channel Attacks

A cryptographic algorithm may be mathematically sound while its implementation leaks information through timing, cache behavior, power consumption, electromagnetic emissions, or fault behavior.

These are **side-channel attacks**.

A MAC implementation that leaks information about its secret key can eventually allow an attacker to forge valid tags. Similarly, comparing MAC tags using an ordinary early-exit string comparison can leak timing information about how many initial bytes are correct.

Cryptographic implementations should therefore use appropriate constant-time techniques and secure key handling.

# Conclusion

Message authentication addresses a fundamental limitation of confidentiality-only systems: an encrypted message can still be modified by an active adversary.

A MAC solves this problem by using a shared secret key to generate a tag that an attacker should not be able to reproduce for a new message. The central security property is **existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack**. The attacker may request valid tags for messages of its choice, but should still be unable to produce a valid tag for a new message except with negligible probability.

PRFs provide a natural theoretical foundation for MAC construction, while PRPs such as block ciphers can be incorporated into practical MAC designs. CBC-MAC, NMAC, PMAC, and HMAC demonstrate different approaches to handling arbitrary-length messages and different performance and security requirements.

Cryptographic hash functions provide a related but distinct family of primitives. They map arbitrary-length inputs to fixed-length outputs and are designed to resist preimage, second-preimage, and collision attacks. Their security properties are not equivalent, and their generic complexities differ significantly.

The Merkle–Damgård construction demonstrates how a fixed-length compression function can process arbitrary-length messages. At the same time, its structure creates properties such as length extension that must be considered when building higher-level cryptographic constructions. HMAC illustrates how careful composition can prevent these structural weaknesses from becoming MAC attacks.

The principal conceptual progression of the chapter is

$$  
\text{Active Attacks}  
\rightarrow  
\text{Integrity}  
\rightarrow  
\text{MACs}  
\rightarrow  
\text{PRF/PRP-Based MACs}  
\rightarrow  
\text{Arbitrary-Length MACs}  
\rightarrow  
\text{Unforgeability}  
\rightarrow  
\text{Hash Functions}  
\rightarrow  
\text{Merkle–Damgård}  
\rightarrow  
\text{Cryptanalytic Attacks}.  
$$

The most important lesson is that cryptographic security is always a property of a precisely defined construction under a precisely defined adversarial model. A secure primitive does not automatically produce a secure protocol, and a hash function with collision resistance is not automatically a MAC. Correct security analysis requires understanding the primitive, its assumptions, its composition, and the exact attacks that the construction is intended to resist.
